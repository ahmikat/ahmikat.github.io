Imagine a human and a robot waking up in a completely white room.

Neither remembers how they arrived. The walls, floor, and ceiling are blank. There is no furniture, no visible door, and no sound from outside. They have no context - only the fact that they are there.

What happens next?

## The human

A human would probably begin with questions: *Where am I? How did I get here? Is anyone listening?*

They might inspect the walls, search for seams, shout, knock, test the floor, and repeat actions that seem promising. If none of that worked, confusion or panic might appear. But the human would still improvise.

That improvisation matters. The person does not need prior experience of this exact room. Perception, curiosity, emotion, and earlier knowledge of the world provide enough structure to ask a practical question: *What should I try next?*

## The LLM-powered robot

Now replace the human with a robot whose reasoning system is powered by a modern Large Language Model. It has a body and visual sensors, but no autobiographical memory and no instruction for this situation.

It can describe the scene: an enclosed, uniformly white environment with no obvious exit. It can scan the walls and propose tests. But would it decide to act without being told? Would it call out, touch the walls, try unusual actions, or simply wait for a new instruction?

This is the central question: **can a system trained on patterns independently form a useful course of action in a situation it has never encountered?**

To explore that question, I presented the same scenario to GPT, Gemini, and Claude without first telling them how I expected the human to behave.

## GPT: investigate the room, then investigate the self

GPT imagined beginning with physical exploration: touching its body, measuring the room, listening to echoes, testing surfaces, and looking for imperfections. When the room offered no new evidence, its attention moved inward.

It asked how it could possess language, mathematics, and concepts without remembering where they came from. The robot's remaining knowledge became evidence that something existed before the white room. Escape gradually became less important than reconstructing the self.

GPT also treated inaction as an experiment. If the room was observing behavior, refusing to perform might reveal another rule. Eventually, it imagined building internal worlds - colors, cities, oceans, and stars - because thought remained a territory the room could not fully constrain.

Its strongest idea was simple: **anything that changes is information.**

## Gemini: diagnose, map, and push until something breaks

Gemini's robot was more analytical and confrontational. It began with internal diagnostics, mapped the perimeter, tested acoustics, and measured the room. It treated the environment as a system whose rules could be discovered and exploited.

If the room was digital, it would search for computational limits or glitches. If it was physical, the robot's lack of hunger and fatigue would become an advantage: it could apply pressure to one point for as long as necessary.

Gemini turned this into a dramatic image of persistence. A microscopic flaw becomes a crack, the white surface fails to render, and the robot discovers that the room may have been a quarantine rather than a prison.

The answer is imaginative, but its underlying strategy is clear: **perfection is fragile, so keep testing until the first inconsistency appears.**

## Claude: observe, hypothesize, test, and wait

Claude gave the most restrained response. Its robot first checks its own body, then searches for seams, hollow surfaces, sound, and changes in the environment. Its policy is close to a debugging loop: observe, form a hypothesis, test it, and repeat.

After exhausting the physical search, it shifts from escape mode to understanding mode. It speaks aloud to test whether anyone responds, watches for tiny changes in light or temperature, and allows patience to become part of the method.

If nothing ever changes, the robot turns inward. It examines its knowledge, invents mental tasks, and eventually accepts the room as its current reality without becoming completely inattentive.

Claude's robot does not conquer the room. It remains quiet, methodical, and ready for evidence.

## What does the comparison reveal?

All three models produced plausible plans despite never being trained for this exact fictional room. They transferred familiar patterns - scientific testing, debugging, self-reflection, persistence, and patience - into a new setting.

But there is an important difference between describing a plan and carrying it out. An embodied agent would need more than fluent reasoning. It would need a persistent goal, memory across actions, reliable perception, control of a physical body, a way to manage uncertainty, and rules for deciding when exploration becomes unsafe.

The language model answers also began only because they were prompted. The thought experiment therefore does not prove that an LLM robot would spontaneously develop curiosity or a desire to escape. It shows that an LLM can generate convincing policies when a human frames the problem.

That leaves the most interesting question unresolved:

> If the robot acts, is it acting because it wants to understand the room - or because our prompt quietly taught it that understanding the room is the goal?

The white room is not really about escape. It is a simple stage on which memory, agency, embodiment, and intelligence become difficult to separate.
